I'd been scheduling the game site's socials through Buffer, and the plan was to move them onto the planner I <Term k="self-hosted">self-host</Term>. Then I sat down to recreate a normal week of posts and ran aground on the first one. Buffer lets you choose an Instagram post, reel or story, tick the box that says a post was made with AI, and pick music from Instagram's library. The planner had post-or-reel as a single option, no AI label, no Facebook reels at all, and a music picker that only ever showed 25 songs. For a game site, reels with music are most of the point.

## Reading the charts first

Before touching anything I read Meta's <Term k="api">API</Term> docs against what the planner actually sends. Most of it was there under the surface. An Instagram media container takes a `media_type` of `REELS` or `STORIES`, a `share_to_feed` flag for reels, and an `is_ai_generated` flag for the label. Facebook Page reels have their own three-step dance: start an upload on `/video_reels`, hand Meta the video's URL in a `file_url` header, then finish with `video_state=PUBLISHED` and the caption. And the music search did get 25 results, because that's the default page size, and it never asked for the next page.

## A fork and a handful of patches

The planner is open source, so I <Term k="fork" def="Your own copy of someone else's code repository, which you can change freely and still pull their updates into.">forked</Term> it and kept the patches small and in the providers where they belong. Instagram's choice is now post, reel or story, with share-to-feed for reels and the AI label on single posts, stories and the parent of a carousel, where Meta wants it rather than on each image. Facebook grew a reel option that walks the three-step upload and publishes on the planner's second pass, once Meta says the video's processed. The music search asks for 100 at a time, follows the `after` cursor up to five pages and drops duplicates. Trending went from 25 songs to 98, which still isn't every song on Instagram, because the API only ever shows a business account a curated slice of the catalogue.

Nine tests cover it, and none of them talk to Meta. The provider's `fetch` is swapped for a <Term k="stub" def="A stand-in for something real, used in tests. It answers like the real thing would and writes down what it was asked.">stub</Term> that records every request, so the tests check the URLs and bodies that would have gone out, including that nothing gets published until the reel's confirmation step comes back.

## Built somewhere else

The <Term k="vps">VPS</Term> the planner runs on is small and already busy, and building a <Term k="monorepo" def="One repository holding several apps and libraries that are built together. Convenient to work in, heavy to build.">monorepo</Term>'s <Term k="docker">Docker</Term> image there would have sunk it for an afternoon. So a tag like `x-hakt-v2.24.0-2` on the fork kicks off a GitHub Actions workflow that runs the tests and then builds an amd64 image and pushes it to the fork's package registry, <Term k="ghcr">ghcr</Term>. On the server, the <Term k="compose">compose</Term> file's image line became `${POSTIZ_IMAGE:-ghcr.io/gitroomhq/postiz-app}:${POSTIZ_VERSION}`, so switching to the patched build is two lines in <Term k="env">`.env`</Term>, and switching back is deleting them. As a side effect the patched image boots in about thirty seconds instead of three and a half minutes, because it doesn't compile anything on start.
