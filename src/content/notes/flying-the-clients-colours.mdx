## Two systems, kept roughly in step

Every agency I have worked with runs two systems. The one where the work
happens, which is a board or a repo or a pile of issues. And the one the client
sees, which is a portal, a shared doc, or a deck at the end of the month.
Someone keeps them roughly aligned by hand. The client's view is always a few
days behind the real one, and nobody is quite sure by how much.

I build client projects in [bosun-x](/locker/bosun-x). It already holds the
honest version: what is done, what is next, what is stuck. [Last
time](/notes/two-gates-and-a-fence) I wrote about the mechanism that lets one
client see their slice of that and nothing else. Two gates, one function, a
build rule that fails if any other code reads the store directly. That part was
finished. This is what got built on top of it: the portal a client actually
logs into.

The thing I did not want to build was a product. A separate application with its
own database, its own board, its own idea of what a task is, that I then feed
from the real one. That is the two-systems problem with extra steps and a sync
job to break quietly.

## Wearing their colours

So the portal is not a separate thing. It is bosun-x. The same image, started a
second time in portal mode, pointed at the same files. What changes is the skin.

An agency gives me a handful of values. A name. An accent colour. A heading font
and a body font. A line for the footer and a link back to their real site. That
is the entire theme. `portal.theagency.com` then looks like `theagency.com`:
their wordmark in the corner, their colour on the buttons, their type on the
page. The layout is mine. The motion is mine. The structure is mine. The clothes
are theirs.

It is deliberately not a page builder. Six values, sensible defaults for
everything else, and a client who signs in sees something that reads as though
their studio sent it, not as though they have been handed a login to my
infrastructure. A client is invited by email address, and that address is the
whole guest list. No shared password, no link that works for whoever it gets
forwarded to.

## What the client actually sees

- **Their projects.** Not the agency's other clients. Not my own half-built
  ideas or the infrastructure notes. A project is invisible until I say, twice,
  that this client may see it.
- **The tasks I chose to show.** A project being visible does not make its tasks
  visible. The fault I am chasing, the awkward bug, the note that says *the last
  developer did something strange here* — those stay on my side unless I
  deliberately hand one over.
- **A summary in my words.** Not a blurb generated from the tech stack. A
  paragraph I write and keep current, in plain language, about where the thing
  really is.
- **Threads they can reply into.** A client can answer on a task or an idea, ask
  a question, and it lands back on my board flagged as needing a look. One click
  to approve or sign off.
- **A direct line.** One always-on message thread per client, separate from any
  project. No ticket, no subject line.
- **What changed since they last looked.** The portal remembers when each client
  last visited and opens with the short list of what has moved.

## There is no publish step

This is the part that matters. I never *publish to the portal*. There is no
export, no build, no push, no "send update" button.

I tick a box on a project that says share this with this client. From that
moment the client is reading the same file I am editing. I add a line to the
status this afternoon and they see it this afternoon. I move a task to done and
it is done in their view too, because it is the same task, in the same file, on
the same disk.

There is no sync job to fail silently. No second database sitting a week behind.
No PDF that was accurate on the first of the month. The client's view cannot
drift from mine, because it *is* mine, with a filter over it and a coat of their
paint.

## The shape of it on the box

The portal is one more service in a compose file. It reads the same data
directory as the operator dashboard, it has its own domain, and it has its own
sign-in, because the way a client gets in should not be the way I get in. My own
login on that deployment still sees every client's project, for a quick check
that a client is seeing what I think they are.

Adding an agency is a service block and about five lines of theme. Adding a
client to that agency is a name, an email address, and the two ticks that share
their first project. There is no onboarding flow because there is almost nothing
to onboard.

## What the client thinks happened

A client signs in to a portal in their own colours, with their projects, a
plain-language note on each, and a line straight to the people building the
thing. It looks bespoke. It looks like their studio stood up a whole client
system for them.

Their studio ticked a box and typed a hex code. Both of those are true at once,
which is the entire idea.
